01

Origins — two schools

Where the style came from, and why it was a school method before it was a look.

The Swiss Style — the International Typographic Style that came out of Zürich and Basel in the 1950s and 1960s — is usually described as a look: Helvetica, red, white space. It is not a look. It is a method, and the method is the grid.

The Swiss Style is usually dated to the 1950s. Its roots reach further back, and it grew in exactly two places. The Swiss government's own history of the style names Basel and Zürich as "the two main centers" — Basel working out of the Basel School of Design, Zürich out of the Zürich School of Arts and Crafts.

Over both sat Ernst Keller, a co-founder of the Swiss Werkbund and, from 1920 to 1956, head of the Zürich graphic design programme. Poster House's exhibition The Swiss Grid calls him "the Father of Swiss Style" and credits his teaching with placing "communication first, focusing on typography over illustration." Keller taught Josef Müller-Brockmann in Zürich and Armin Hofmann in Basel — the two figures the style is published through, and the split between them is the style's split. The same two sources describe the difference plainly: Zürich "championed the use of layout grids," while Basel "used layout grids more selectively."

The style's journal was Neue Grafik — a trilingual Zürich publication edited by Richard Paul Lohse, Hans Neuburg, Müller-Brockmann and Carlo Vivarelli, running from 1958 to 1965. Its first issue, in September 1958, was designed by Vivarelli and set in Akzidenz-Grotesk.

Two details are worth carrying forward. First, the grid predates the style: the Swiss government credits Max Bill with creating "the first true layout grid system in the late 1930s." Second, the style kept going after its moment — from 1978, Müller-Brockmann built the Swiss Federal Railways visual identity with Peter Spalinger, a white double-arrow on red, which remains the most durable Swiss application of a single accent.

Third, the style had a launch. In 1949 Max Bill conceived Die gute Form, an international touring exhibition for the Swiss Werkbund, shown first at the Mustermesse Basel and the Kunstgewerbemuseum Zürich — a programme of good design that turned two schools' teaching into something with a public address.

02

The grid, anatomised

Columns, gutter, baseline, modulus — the four parts and what each one is for.

Every account of this style routes through one book: Müller-Brockmann's Grid Systems in Graphic Design — the German original appeared as Raster Systeme für die grafische Gestaltung, and the editions are dated differently across sources, so cite the edition you are reading. Its systems run from 8 to 32 fields, the columns. Worth remembering: twelve columns is a web convention, not a Swiss one.

Four parts do the work. Columns divide the page horizontally. Gutters separate them. The baseline is the horizontal line each line of type sits on, and the leading between two lines is always a whole multiple of it. The modulus is the base unit everything else divides into — margins, measures, leading, and the size of the type itself.

Poster House's exhibition shows what this looked like in practice: a "flexible grid" adapted across a series, a three-column structure paired with "precisely-measured geometric images," two columns set in differing weights, a "monolithic block of solid text" — and the exceptions that prove the rule. Karl Gerstner's "programmes," "highly complex and innovative" grids. A grid tilted to 50 degrees, "providing a visual tension that not only echoes, but also enhances the message."

note

"The grid system is an aid, not a guarantee. It permits a number of possible uses and each designer can look for a solution appropriate to his personal style." — Josef Müller-Brockmann

tip

"But one must learn how to use the grid; it is an art that requires practice." A grid does not make a page good. It makes the decisions on the page visible, which is a different and more useful thing.

The grid's proof is the poster. Müller-Brockmann made more than seventy for the Tonhalle-Gesellschaft Zürich between 1951 and 1972. The best known is the Beethoven of 1955 — for the concert of Tuesday 22 February, conducted by Carl Schuricht — and it is the clearest demonstration of what the grid is for.

It contains no picture. There is no photograph, no drawing, no ornament: a field of black on white, cut by large concentric arcs generated from a single centre, each band a different width, sweeping off the top and right edges. The subject is beethoven, set in bold lowercase flush left at mid-height, with the concert details below in a narrow column of small lowercase sans, ragged right, and a single line of credit set vertically against the right edge. The arcs are not decoration laid over the type. They are the grid's own geometry, and the type is aligned to them.

03

Typography as method

The objective voice: hierarchy by size and weight, alignment to the grid, nothing decorative.

"Objective typography" gets used loosely. Poster House's exhibition states the rules concretely, and they are stricter than the phrase suggests. The style's principal sans families were Akzidenz Grotesk Bold, Univers 65 Bold and Helvetica Bold — one weight of three faces, set large. Parentheses went in "a slightly smaller point size than the rest of the text." Red appeared in "three different sizes, creating a visual force."

One rule is quietly radical, and it connects straight back to the Bauhaus: lowercase-only composition, with no capitals even on proper nouns. The exhibition's note is that this "condenses leading and kerning to produce a weightier block of information" — the same move Herbert Bayer made at Dessau a quarter-century earlier, arriving by a different route.

01

Build hierarchy from size and weight, not from ornament. A handful of sizes, used without exception. Nothing is bold, coloured or boxed to make a point that size could have made.

02

Set flush left, ragged right. Never justify. The rag is a structural element, not a flaw to be ironed out.

03

Snap the small stuff too. Richard Paul Lohse locked the right side of his numerals to the grid line; Gerstner let a numeral's tail bleed past the measure so its edge still ran along the same grid as the body of the number.

04

Use one face and one weight per role. The style had three sans families and used one weight of each. A second weight is a second opinion about the hierarchy, and the hierarchy only gets one.

04

The typefaces

Akzidenz-Grotesk, Helvetica, Univers — three grotesques and the jobs each one did.

Helvetica and Univers were both released commercially in 1957. Akzidenz-Grotesk, the Berthold Foundry's release, is generally dated to 1958. The three are close cousins — all grotesques, drawn for the same job of carrying information without decoration — and the differences between them are the differences between the two schools that used them.

Here the sources disagree, and the disagreement is instructive. The Swiss government's history says Zürich leaned toward Helvetica and Basel toward Univers. Poster House's exhibition agrees about Basel — "Basel favored Univers experimentation and looser grid adherence" — but puts Zürich with Akzidenz-Grotesk and "strict adherence to the grid." Poster House puts it plainly: Helvetica is the style's biggest export, but "no posters in this show feature Helvetica."

What the style did with type is stranger than the choice of face. Univers 65 Bold was the workhorse. Red was set in three different sizes to create visual force. And compositions were set lowercase-only, without capitals on proper nouns, which condensed the leading and the kerning and produced — in the exhibition's phrase — "a weightier block of information."

05

Black, white, one red

Why the palette is a discipline and not an absence of choice.

The Swiss government describes the style as pursuing "a design language reduced to its essentials," built on "the use of photography and graphic symbols, sparing utilisation of colour, sans serif fonts and asymmetrical layouts." The operative word is sparing. This is not a black-and-white style with red in it — it is a style that spent colour carefully, which in practice meant one colour used as a field rather than an ornament.

The most durable instance is not a poster. From 1978, Müller-Brockmann and Peter Spalinger built the Swiss Federal Railways identity around a white double-arrow on red: a single accent doing the work of an entire visual system, legible at platform scale and at ticket scale.

The discipline is also visible in what the style refused. The Beethoven poster — the most reproduced artefact of the movement — is entirely black and white. Not black, white and a spot of red: no colour at all, on a poster measuring roughly 70 by 100 centimetres, seen from across a street. The red arrives later, and by way of a corporate identity rather than a concert poster.

Inside the compositions, red was handled as a scale, not a swatch — set in three different sizes to create visual force — and Hofmann's 1959 sculptors' exhibition poster shows the photographic half of the style working alongside it. The two poles are usually presented as opposites: Müller-Brockmann's constructed typography against Hofmann's photo-montage. In practice they were one discipline applied to two materials.

tip

The discipline is the rarity. One red used at three sizes beats three colours used at one. If red appears on every callout, every badge and every link, then the one accent that was carrying the hierarchy has been spent.

06

The grid on the web

CSS Grid, the baseline grid, modular scales, and the 8-point unit.

CSS Grid is the Swiss grid's descendant, and the spec inherits its vocabulary directly. grid-template-columns is Baseline "Widily available" — across browsers since October 2017 — and repeat() exists, in MDN's wording, to let "a large number of columns that exhibit a recurring pattern to be written in a more compact form." The fr unit specifies "the track's flex factor," so that each track "takes a share of the remaining space in proportion to its flex factor."

That is the whole translation, and it is exact. A Swiss grid was a division of a page into columns with a fixed relationship between that division and the type. A CSS grid is a division of a container into tracks with a fixed relationship between that division and the content. Everything else — the fluid column, the proportional gutter, the collapsed layout on narrow screens — is bookkeeping the browser does for you.

/* the page grid: twelve columns, one gutter, one page width */
.pg{display:grid;grid-template-columns:repeat(12,1fr);gap:var(--s7) var(--gut);max-width:1240px}
.sc{grid-column:1/span 8}               /* the main column — grid columns 1–8 */
.toc{grid-column:10/span 3;grid-row:2}  /* the rail — grid columns 10–12 */
p{line-height:1.5} /* 16px × 1.5 = 24px — one baseline */

The baseline grid is the part CSS does not give you. There is no baseline-grid feature. What you get is a convention: set the body to 16px with a line-height of 1.5, so every line of body text advances exactly 24px and every baseline lands on a 24px rhythm. Then remove every margin, padding and border that is not a multiple of 24. A repeating-linear-gradient background can draw the baseline grid, but it does not snap anything to it — it only makes misalignment visible. Baseline alignment is maintained by arithmetic, not by the renderer.

The type scale does the same job vertically. Tim Brown's modular scale, first introduced at Build 2010, defines a scale as "one base with one ratio" — "Bases are a scale's root numbers," and "Ratios are multiplied by the base to produce a scale of numbers that are proportionally related." The named ratios run from the minor second at 1.067 through the major third at 1.25 and the perfect fourth at 1.333, to the golden section at 1.618. Anything beyond a base and a ratio is, in the site's phrasing, a "multi-stranded modular scale."

Spacing has its own arithmetic. Material Design 3's spacing tokens run 0, 4dp, 8dp, 12dp, 16dp, 24dp, 32dp, 40dp, 48dp, 56dp, 64dp — a 4dp base with 8dp steps above 16dp. That is the 8-point grid as it exists in practice today, and it is why a 24px baseline and a 4px spacing scale can share a page: the baseline is a multiple of the spacing unit. The 8-point grid is popularly credited to Google's Material Design and the designer Elliot Dahl, popularised through the 2010s and now standard across Material, Carbon and Ant Design — though newer systems are drifting back toward 4-point grids.

What is genuinely new is not the grid. It is that the grid is now invisible by default — the browser lays it out for you — which means the discipline has to move somewhere else. It moves to the decisions the grid cannot make for you: which sizes, which weights, how much space, and where the one accent goes.

07

Where it breaks

Justified text, fixed pages, thin weights, and what the style's politics were.

Justified text. Swiss print set flush left and ragged right. Justification requires hyphenation and produces rivers — and it removes the rag, which was the point. On screen it is worse, because the measure changes with the viewport.

Fixed page formats. An A-series poster had one page. A screen has a thousand, each a different width. "Strict adherence to the grid" means nothing if the grid is re-derived at every viewport.

The accessibility floor. WCAG 2.2 puts hard numbers on all of it. Contrast (Minimum), SC 1.4.3, Level AA, requires 4.5:1 for body text and 3:1 for large-scale text — 18pt and above non-bold, or 14pt and above bold. Non-text Contrast, SC 1.4.11, requires 3:1 for the visual boundaries of user-interface components and for graphics needed to understand the content: a red rule that carries meaning is a graphic, and it has to clear 3:1 against the paper. Target Size (Minimum), SC 2.5.8, Level AA, requires pointer targets of at least 24 by 24 CSS pixels, with exceptions for spacing, inline targets, user-agent-controlled sizing and essential presentation.

Text Spacing is the one that breaks rigid grids. SC 1.4.12 requires that content survives, without loss, when line height is raised to at least 1.5 times the font size, paragraph spacing to at least 2 times, letter spacing to at least 0.12 times and word spacing to at least 0.16 times. Every fixed-height row and every fixed-height card in a print-derived grid violates that. Reflow, SC 1.4.10, requires content to work at a width of 320 CSS pixels without two-dimensional scrolling; Resize Text, SC 1.4.4, requires 200 percent text without loss of content or function. A grid with hard-coded row heights fails both.

warn

Swiss Style design is usually taught as if it has no political content. It does. The "objective" voice, the absence of authorial expression, the rational grid — these were deliberate choices about what a designed object should be for, and they descend directly from the 1930s reaction to propaganda typography. Treat the style as a political artifact, not a neutral tool.

08

Working rules

The checklist that falls out of the above.

01

Divide the page before you set the type. Twelve columns, one gutter, one baseline. Choose the unit first and let every measure derive from it.

02

Build the hierarchy from size and weight alone. Seven sizes at most. Nothing is bold, coloured or boxed to make a point that size could have made.

03

Snap the small stuff too. Numeral edges, caption rules, the gap under a heading. Alignment that holds for the headline and fails for the table is not alignment.

04

Spend the accent sparingly, and at three sizes. One colour, used as a field, a rule and a word — not as a fill on everything that needs emphasis.

05

Let the grid hold the layout, and the numbers hold the reader. Contrast at 4.5:1, targets at 24px, reflow at 320px, text spacing that survives a user's override. The method is only worth adopting if the result is readable by everyone.
